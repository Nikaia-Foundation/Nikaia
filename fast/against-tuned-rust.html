Very fast

1BRC study: the Nikaia compiler vs. human vs. AI

1. What the study asks

The One Billion Row Challenge reads a file of weather readings, one station;temperature a line, and prints each station's minimum, mean and maximum. It is small enough to read in one sitting and hard enough that people have spent months on the fastest answer: the input has to be searched, hashed, parsed and counted with almost nothing wasted.

The study asks three questions of Nikaia, which is pre-alpha:

  • What does the compiler make of a plain program? examples/1brc.nika is written the way the problem reads: a grammar for a line, a fold over the rows. Making it fast is the compiler's job.
  • What does hand-tuned Nikaia reach today? fast.nika uses the same building blocks as the fastest Rust programs, with no unsafe: every index and overflow is proved or checked. fast-unsafe.nika is the same program with the claims about the input written unsafe { assume(…) }, believed rather than checked.
  • Can Nikaia reach the hardware as far as tuned Rust? That is what fast-unsafe.nika is for. It does not try to be cleverer than the people who wrote the fastest Rust programs; it uses their tricks. It shows that every one of them can be written in Nikaia: a search a block at a time, a name read as words, a branch-free parse, an instruction chosen by the CPU level the program is built for, and claims about the input, each one assume the reader sees.
  • Where does the journey go? The gap between the safe and the unsafe program is what the prover still has to learn. Every step it learns moves the safe program, and every other Nikaia program, without a line of them changed.

Along the way the study shows what Rust can do in the hands of a person and of an AI assistant, who leads, and which tricks put each program where it is.

2. Method

Every program prints the same output, byte for byte, for the same million rows. Counted with callgrind (valgrind --tool=callgrind --branch-sim=yes), never timed: a count is the work a program does, and it does not move from one day to the next. For a program that uses all cores it is the work of all of them. Release builds on every side with the same settings, 16 codegen units, at the baseline x86-64 and at x86-64-v3 (AVX2, BMI2). Length in tokens, the same rule for both languages. The full method and every row are in benches/brc.

3. Results

  • Safe Nikaia leads at the baseline. At x86-64, fast.nika does 12 % less work a row than the fastest Rust a person wrote and mispredicts under a third as often (0.14 against 0.47). Against the AI assistant's best it does 21 % less work and mispredicts well under half as often. It has no unsafe and a ninth of the human program's tokens.
  • Unsafe Nikaia shows what is left to prove. fast-unsafe.nika does 15 % less work than the human program. The 4.7 instructions between it and the safe program are checks the prover cannot yet remove.
  • At x86-64-v3 the human program runs the fewest instructions: 91.5 a row against 94.3 for unsafe Nikaia and 100.9 for safe Nikaia. It mispredicts twice as often as either (0.30 against 0.14). A mispredicted branch costs a current core about as long as 50 instructions take; weighed that way, the human program and unsafe Nikaia are about even. It parses two rows' temperatures in one AVX2 vector; section 4 shows the rest.
  • The plain program is in the field of the human programs: 209 against 208 and 216, in 642 tokens against several thousand, with the whole file checked as UTF-8 and every index and overflow proved or checked.

4. The techniques, and who uses them

Finding the separator 64 bytes at a time

All fast programs find the ; by comparing 16 to 64 bytes at once and reading the hits from a bit mask. In Rust that is intrinsics inside unsafe. In Nikaia it is bytes::find_all over a ByteSet: the standard library does the block compare, and each next hit costs one tzcnt. A set written in the program is fixed when compiling, so no block pays for a choice. One option says how to go from one hit to the next: through the block's mask (the default), or by comparing the 32 bytes right after the last hit (scan: bytes::Scan::AfterHit), which the fast programs take because a row is about 14 bytes long.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika · beowolx · AI variant 2

Reading a station's name as two words

The first sixteen bytes of a name, read as two 64-bit words, are both its hash and its key; the bytes are compared only for longer names. Nikaia reads them with u64_le from a padded copy of the input's end, so no read can run past it, and the bounds are proved, not trusted.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika · beowolx · AI variant 2

Parsing a temperature without a branch

The position of the . follows from the word's bits, and the value from shifts and one multiply. beowolx goes further at x86-64-v3: it parses the temperatures of two rows in one AVX2 vector.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika · beowolx · AI variant 2

Comparing text keys a word at a time

Every Nikaia map is the standard library's own type over hashbrown's table. Where its key is text, it hashes the first sixteen bytes inline and compares a key a word at a time, the trick the fast programs write by hand; Rust's &str == calls memcmp every time. It took the plain program from 222.9 to 208.8 instructions a row without a line of it changed.

Used by1brc.nika · every Nikaia program with a map of text

Proving checks instead of making them

Nikaia keeps every index and overflow check unless it proves the check cannot fail. The prover carries facts through loops, keeps a type's rule as a fact where no method can break it, proves a function's and a method's conditions where they are called, and bounds shifts and sums. That took fast.nika from 163.6 to 102.7 instructions a row, before it traded some of them for fewer mispredicted branches (below); the program only states, in a few asserts, what it relies on.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika · 1brc.nika (its temperature and counter checks)

Letting the programmer vouch for the input

unsafe { assume(claim) } states what the input's format guarantees: a name of at most 100 bytes, fewer than 10 000 stations. Built with checks removed, each claim is believed and the checks it implies are gone; built with checks on, each is checked. nikaia check --asserts lists every claim.

Used byfast-unsafe.nika

Keeping the rare path out of the loop

A station's first row copies its name into the table, once a station. Marked @inline(never), that code stays out of the loop that runs once a row, and the loop keeps its values in registers: 6.7 instructions a row.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika

Checking UTF-8 once a name, not once a byte

The fast Nikaia programs read the file as bytes and check a station's name as UTF-8 once, where it is printed. The Rust programs check nothing. The plain Nikaia program checks the whole file, about 10 instructions a row.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika

Using every core with one line

The plain program is a grammar and a fold; par_fold cuts the file and merges the pieces, and whether it runs on one core or all of them is one line in nikaia.toml. The human programs write their threads by hand.

Used by1brc.nika · fast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika

Trading a few instructions for fewer mispredicted branches

A branch whose outcome varies with the input is mispredicted often, and each miss costs a current core about as long as 50 instructions take. fast.nika cuts a name's words with low_bits instead of two branches on its length, and looks for each ; right after the last instead of where a block's hits run out. That costs 13 instructions a row at x86-64 and brings the mispredicted branches from 0.92 to 0.14 a row.

Used byfast.nika · fast-unsafe.nika · AI variant 2 (a cmov for the name's length)

An instruction chosen by the CPU level

Where the CPU level the program is built for has crc32 (x86-64-v3), fast-unsafe.nika hashes a name's two words with two of them; elsewhere it multiplies. comptime and target::has decide that while the program is built, so the other hash is not in the binary.

Used byfast-unsafe.nika · beowolx · AI variant 2

Why the human program pulls ahead at x86-64-v3

beowolx gains 40 instructions a row from x86-64 to x86-64-v3, the Nikaia programs 15 to 17. About 16 of beowolx's 40 come from a hash that collides less once crc32 is there: its baseline build is weak, and the gain recovers from that. About 22 come from code it compiles only for that level: the two-row AVX2 temperature parse, bzhi to mask the name, an SSE update of the stats.

Nikaia's programs now take two of the three: low_bits is one bzhi at x86-64-v3, and fast-unsafe.nika hashes with crc32. Nikaia's own vector types (simd::Fixed[u64, 2]) can write the two-row parse, but measured it costs more than the scalar parse: the lowering does not yet keep the two lanes in one register. Even written with intrinsics it saved only 1.6 instructions a row, so the rest of beowolx's lead at this level lies elsewhere.

Used bybeowolx; the two-row parse measured and not yet worth it in fast.nika and fast-unsafe.nika
